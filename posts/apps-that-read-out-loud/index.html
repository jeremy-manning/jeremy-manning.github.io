Apps That Read Out Loud

Jeremy Manning

August 19, 2026

A note on authorship. This brief was researched and written entirely by Claude.ai. I commissioned it, set the requirements, and reviewed it before publishing, but none of the prose below is mine. I want to be clear about where I’m using AI rather than leave anyone guessing.

What this was measured against

Every read-aloud tool that surfaces in a normal search is a subscription service with an account wall. That is not a technical constraint — local speech synthesis has been solved for years — it is a billing decision. Cloud TTS meters nicely; on-device TTS does not. The search was for the exception.

TTS here means text-to-speech: software that converts written text into spoken audio. The requirements:

  • Privacy — no account to create, nothing uploaded anywhere
  • Cost model — one-time purchase, not SaaS
  • Processing — fully on-device, works offline
  • Input, phase one — Markdown files
  • Input, phase two — PDFs, including scanned pages via OCR
  • Output — runs on iOS, or exports an audio file to listen to later

What the machines do today, for nothing

Before spending anything, both devices already ship a complete offline speech engine. On the Mac in question, 184 voices were installed — but every premium voice was missing, which means the only voices available were the low-quality compact ones. That alone may account for the impression that built-in TTS is not good enough.

Download the good voices first. The ones marked Premium are roughly 500 MB each and genuinely competitive with paid neural voices. Ava, Evan and Zoe are the best of them.

  • macOS › System Settings › Accessibility › Spoken Content › System Voice › Manage Voices
  • iOS › Settings › Accessibility › Spoken Content › Voices

On iOS, free: Shortcuts › Gallery › Make Spoken Audio from Text. Generates an .m4a from any text using on-device voices and saves it to Files. Fully offline, no account — and it covers the export-and-listen-later workflow at zero cost.

On macOS, free: the say command reads and exports already, with no software to install.

say -f notes.md
say -f notes.md -o out.aiff

The honest test: premium voices plus the Shortcuts action may cover most of the need for nothing. If they do, the paid app below is buying the library, resume-position memory and PDF layout handling on top of a foundation that already works — which is still worth it, but worth knowing in that order.

Verdict: buy Speech Central

If the free options are not enough, one app met every requirement, and it is cheap.

Speech Central, $9.99, one-time. Free to download with a single $9.99 Pro unlock. Not a subscription. Rated 4.59 across 1,534 ratings, and actively maintained — the version examined shipped a week before this brief.

  • Reads Markdown natively, alongside PDF, EPUB, DOCX, PPTX, HTML, TXT and RTF — no conversion step for phase one.
  • Handles scanned PDFs with two-column layout detection. This is the genuinely hard part of the problem, and it is already solved.
  • Exports audio files — added in v17.7.2 on 12 August 2026, which is why older comparisons do not mention it. Item context menu → Export Audio File.
  • Uses Apple system voices offline. The listing states plainly: no ads, no tracking, no usage analytics.
  • Runs on iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple Watch, Apple TV and Vision — so iOS is native, and the audio export is a bonus rather than a workaround.

One asterisk: iOS and macOS are separate purchases at $9.99 each. Both together is still under $20, once.

The obvious answer is no longer an answer

For about a decade the standard recommendation for this exact need was Voice Dream Reader: roughly $15 one-time, and it read anything you gave it.

It has since been absorbed by NaturalReader and now ships as Voice Dream – Natural Reader. A subscription is required to use the app — $4.99/month up to $79.99/year — iCloud sync is mandatory for the library, and AI chat and summarisation have been added on top. It has become the precise thing this search was trying to escape.

A stale Voice Dream Reader - Education listing still sits in the App Store at $119.99, last updated November 2023. It is an abandoned institutional SKU. Do not buy it.

Why the list is this short

Around a dozen other apps surfaced. Most were excluded because the sources promoting them were the vendors themselves. The “best offline TTS of 2026” roundup is now a near-complete SEO genre: several apps that ranked first in these listicles turned out to be publishing the listicles on their own domains.

One app was presented as the category winner. Checking the App Store directly showed a single version from March 2026 with four ratings averaging 2.25. Others carried a single rating, or none at all. Verifying against Apple’s own API rather than the roundups reversed the ranking almost completely, which is why the table above is short and why every app in it has thousands of independent ratings behind it.

Worth noting for later: the strongest of the unvetted apps run Kokoro-82M on-device — an Apache-2.0 model whose voice quality clearly beats Apple’s built-in voices. The technology is real even where the products are unproven, and it is the obvious upgrade path if system voices disappoint.

The build option, parked

Building this was considered and deferred. The finding worth keeping: speech synthesis is the easy part. On macOS, AVSpeechSynthesizer, PDFKit and the Vision framework are all free system APIs with no dependencies, and Vision’s on-device OCR outperforms Tesseract.

  • A weekend gets a working Markdown reader with play, pause, speed and sentence highlighting — the last of these comes free from a system callback that reports the character range being spoken.
  • A week or two more covers PDFs, where the real difficulty is not extraction but reading order: multi-column academic papers, running heads, footnotes and citation markers all need filtering before they hit the voice.
  • Swapping in Kokoro improves the audio but costs the word-timing callbacks, since it emits a bare waveform with no alignment data — so word-level highlighting would have to be dropped or rebuilt via forced alignment.

Which is the case against building: the work is mostly re-solving PDF text extraction, a tedious rather than novel problem, and a $9.99 app has already solved it. The case for building is control, and independence from a small vendor staying alive.
