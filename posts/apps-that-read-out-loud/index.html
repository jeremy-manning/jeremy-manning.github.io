Apps That Read Out Loud

Jeremy Manning

August 19, 2026

I wanted something simple: an app that would read my documents out loud to me. Text files to start with, PDFs later on, including the kind you get from scanning or photographing a page.

Text-to-speech just means software that reads written words aloud in a synthetic voice. Some of it runs on a company’s computers over the internet. Some of it runs entirely on your own phone or laptop. That difference turned out to be the whole story.

The problem is that almost everything you find when you search is the first kind. You make an account, you hand over your documents, and you pay every month for as long as you use it. That is not because reading text aloud is difficult or expensive. Your phone has been able to do it for years. It is because a monthly fee is easier to build a business on than a one-time sale.

So I went looking for the exception. Here is what I wanted:

  • No account to create, and nothing uploaded anywhere
  • Pay once, not every month
  • Runs on my own device, with no internet needed
  • Reads plain text now, PDFs and scanned pages later
  • Works on my iPhone, or saves an audio file I can listen to later

Start with what you already own

Before spending anything, it is worth knowing that both the Mac and the iPhone already include a complete reading-aloud system at no cost.

When I checked my own Mac, I had 184 voices installed and not one of the good ones. The only voices available were the flat, robotic ones, which probably explains why I had assumed for years that the built-in option was not good enough.

Download the good voices first. The ones marked “Premium” are about 500 MB each and sound genuinely close to the paid apps. Ava, Evan and Zoe are the best of them.

  • On a Mac: System Settings › Accessibility › Spoken Content › System Voice › Manage Voices
  • On an iPhone: Settings › Accessibility › Spoken Content › Voices

On the iPhone, there is a free shortcut that already does the job. Open the Shortcuts app, go to the Gallery, and add Make Spoken Audio from Text. It turns any text into an audio file and saves it into your Files app. No internet, no account, no cost, and it covers the “listen to it later” idea completely.

On the Mac, one line in Terminal will do it. Nothing to install:

say -f notes.txt
say -f notes.txt -o out.aiff

Good voices plus that free shortcut may cover most of what you need for nothing at all. I would try that before buying anything.

The app worth buying

If the free options are not enough, one app met every requirement, and it is cheap.

Speech Central, $9.99, paid once. The app is free to download with a single $9.99 unlock for the full version. There is no subscription. It has over 1,500 ratings averaging 4.59 out of 5, and it is still being actively improved.

  • It opens almost any kind of document: plain text, PDFs, e-books, Word files, PowerPoint files and web pages
  • It handles scanned and photographed pages, including ones printed in two columns like a journal article. This is the genuinely hard part of the problem, and it is already solved
  • It saves audio files, so you can listen later like a podcast episode. This arrived in August 2026, which is why older comparison articles do not mention it
  • It works with no internet connection, and promises no ads, no tracking, and no collection of usage data
  • It runs on iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple Watch, Apple TV and Vision

One catch: the iPhone and Mac versions are sold separately at $9.99 each. Both together is still under $20, once, forever.

The obvious choice stopped being an option

For about ten years the standard recommendation for this was an app called Voice Dream Reader. You paid roughly $15 once, and it read anything you gave it.

It has since been bought by another company and relaunched as Voice Dream – Natural Reader. It now requires a subscription, between $4.99 a month and $79.99 a year. Your library is stored on the company’s servers rather than your device, and various artificial-intelligence features have been added on top. It has become exactly the sort of thing I was trying to avoid.

There is also still an old listing in the App Store called Voice Dream Reader - Education, priced at $119.99, which has not been updated since November 2023. It is an abandoned version sold to schools. Do not buy it.

Why only three apps are on that list

Around a dozen others came up, and most got left out for one reason: the glowing reviews recommending them turned out to be written by the companies that make them. Several “best apps of 2026” articles were published on the app makers’ own websites, ranking themselves first.

One app was presented that way as the clear winner of the whole category. Checking the App Store directly showed it had been released only a few months earlier and had four ratings, averaging 2.25 out of 5. Others had a single rating, or none at all.

Going to Apple’s own records instead of the review articles reversed the rankings almost completely. That is the part I would pass on to anyone doing this kind of search: the top results are increasingly written by the people selling the thing, and the ratings count is the fastest way to tell.

What about building one?

I did look at whether it would be worth making my own instead. The surprising part is that making a computer speak is the easy half, and Apple gives that away free to anyone writing software.

The hard half is untangling a PDF so the words come out in the right order, without page numbers and footnotes being read aloud in the middle of a sentence. That is the part a $9.99 app has already spent years getting right, which is a pretty good argument for simply buying it.

What I would do

  1. Download the Premium voices on both the Mac and the iPhone. Free, takes a few minutes.
  2. Try the free Shortcuts action on the iPhone with a page or two of text.
  3. If that is not enough, buy Speech Central for the iPhone for $9.99, and add the Mac version only if you want it there too.
  4. Skip anything that asks you to create an account or pay monthly. There is no longer a good reason to accept either one.
